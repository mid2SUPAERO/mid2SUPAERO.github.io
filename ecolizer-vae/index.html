Mapping the Ecolizer

Eco-indicators are numbers in a booklet. A small variational autoencoder turns them into a map you can colour, contour and walk across, and shows exactly where such a map stops being trustworthy.

This article continues Ashby's Maps and Ashby's Maps, Renewed. There, the price axis of a classical materials chart was replaced by an environmental one, and a variational autoencoder (VAE) replaced the two-axis chart by a continuous map. Here we apply both ideas to real environmental data: the Ecolizer 2.0 , a booklet published by the Flemish waste agency OVAM for designers who want to estimate the environmental impact of a product. Every figure on this page is interactive and runs in your browser; the models were trained in a companion notebook.

Reading guide. Sections 1 to 3 need no machine learning: they are a tour of the data and of the eco-Ashby indices. Sections 4 to 6 introduce the VAE and let you play with it. Section 7 is the one to read twice: it says what the map cannot do.

1 · What is an eco-indicator?

The Ecolizer reduces the life-cycle assessment of each material, process and energy carrier to one number, the eco-indicator, in millipoints (mPt). One point (Pt) is one thousandth of the yearly environmental burden of an average European, so 1 mPt is a millionth of it. The number is computed with the ReCiPe method , which follows 19 mid-point impact categories (acidification, ozone depletion, toxicity, land use, resource depletion...) up to three damage categories, and weights them human health 400 : ecosystems 400 : raw materials 200. The booklet insists on two caveats that we will carry through this whole article: only relative comparisons matter, and "these numbers have a relatively high uncertainty; small differences between scores must be treated with particular care".

For a material, the booklet gives four kinds of numbers, all per kilogram unless stated otherwise: the production indicator (extraction to factory gate), the recycling block (process impact, the credit for the primary material that is not produced, and their sum), the waste-treatment indicator for an average European scenario (80 % landfill, 20 % incineration), and a list of processing indicators (injection moulding, turning, anodising...). Energy and transport carry their own units (per kWh, per MJ, per tonne-kilometre).

Provenance. Impact numbers come from the Ecolizer PDF (the steel sheet is a raster image and was typed by hand; wood panels and concrete, given per m³, were converted with an assumed density). Density ρ and Young's modulus E are not in the Ecolizer. For 118 materials we added rounded typical handbook values, marked "typical" wherever they appear. Everything built on them is only as good as these approximations.

Three things are worth noticing before any modelling. First, the scale: platinum-group metals and mercury sit two to five orders of magnitude above typical engineering materials, which is why the choice of a catalyst or a contact material can dominate a product's whole footprint. Second, for most solid materials production dominates waste treatment; end of life matters mainly for materials that are cheap to produce, like wood and minerals. Third, the same kilogram can differ by a factor of twenty depending on the route: primary aluminium is 1 045 mPt/kg, secondary aluminium from new scrap only 45.

2 · From impact per kilogram to impact per function

A designer does not buy kilograms; they buy stiffness, strength or insulation. In the Ashby method the material that delivers a given function with least mass is the one that minimises a material index built from the properties. Multiply the mass by the production indicator and you get an eco-index, the environmental analogue of the price-based index . For a part that must reach a given stiffness, with the free geometry variable eliminated,

I_a \;=\; \frac{\text{prod}\cdot\rho}{E^{a}}, \qquad a = 1\ (\text{tie}),\quad a=\tfrac12\ (\text{beam}),\quad a=\tfrac13\ (\text{panel}),

and on a log–log chart of eco-cost per volume ($$\text{prod}\cdot\rho$$) against $$E$$ the contour $$I_a=\text{const}$$ is a straight line of slope $$a$$. Minimising $$I_a$$ means sliding that line down and to the right until it touches the best material.

Production is only one life-cycle phase. In the booklet's own worked examples the use phase dwarfs everything: a coffee machine kept warm twice a day for five years costs 11 625 mPt of electricity (375 kWh at 31 mPt/kWh) against 793 mPt for all the materials. The Ecolizer therefore also lists the electricity mix of each country, heat sources and transport modes, which is the second dataset of this article.

3 · Why a map?

Figures 1 and 2 show one or two quantities at a time. A real material carries at least five numbers for us: production, waste, recycling gain, density and stiffness. The ranking changes with every question you ask (cheapest to produce? lightest per function? best to recycle?) and some entries do not have all the numbers: the booklet marks them dna (data not available), and only 25 of our 193 materials have a full recycling block. A classical chart simply drops the points that are missing a coordinate.

A variational autoencoder offers a different deal. It learns to compress each material into a code of two numbers $$z=(z_1,z_2)$$ from which a decoder can rebuild the five properties. Because the code is drawn from a smooth, continuous distribution, the decoder is a smooth function over the whole plane: every property becomes a continuous field, the neighbourhood of a material is meaningful, and we can differentiate through the decoder, which is what makes the gradient search of Section 6 possible. This is the same idea as in Ashby's Maps, Renewed; the data are different, and so are the limits.

4 · The model

Inputs. For the 118 materials with a density and a modulus we use five numbers: $$\log_{10}\text{prod}$$, $$\log_{10}\text{waste}$$, the recycling gain $$g=-\text{total}/\text{prod}$$ (the fraction of the production impact recovered by closed-loop recycling), $$\log_{10}\rho$$ and $$\log_{10}E$$, each standardised. Waste treatment is missing for 34 of them and recycling gain for 100, so the model handles missing values : the encoder receives the values with the missing entries set to zero plus a 0/1 mask, and the reconstruction error counts only the observed entries. During training we also hide features at random, which teaches the encoder to produce a sensible code from whatever subset a new material comes with. The 75 other entries (chemicals, glues, raw sands, materials for which stiffness is meaningless) are not used for training but can still be placed on the map by the encoder.

Objective. We use the β-VAE loss : a masked squared reconstruction error plus β times the Kullback–Leibler divergence between the encoder's distribution and the standard normal prior,

\mathcal L \;=\; \sum_{j} m_j\,(x_j-\hat x_j)^2 \;+\; \beta\;\tfrac12\sum_{k=1}^{2}\big(\mu_k^2+\sigma_k^2-\log\sigma_k^2-1\big),

with $$m_j=1$$ for observed entries and 0 for missing ones. The KL weight is ramped up from zero over the first 30 % of training to avoid an early collapse of the code . The parameter β sets the compromise: with a small β the VAE reconstructs the training data nearly perfectly but the plane between the points is a patchwork; with a large β the map is smooth and blurred; at β = 4 it collapses completely to the prior and learns nothing. We keep five models, β from 0.05 to 2, and use β = 0.5 by default.

5 · The latent map

Place every material at the position its encoder assigns it, and colour the background by what the decoder says at each point. The map was never told the families, but neighbours are usually from the same family: a leave-one-out 3-nearest-neighbour classifier on the two latent coordinates identifies the family of a material correctly 76 % of the time, against 16 % for always guessing the largest family. The platinum-group metals, the elastomers, the insulation foams and the thermoplastics each form a recognisable island, and the minerals and ceramics sit together in the lower left.

With the index maps selected, the green region of low eco-impact per unit of function occupies the lower left (minerals, concrete, brick) and stretches towards wood; the red region on the right belongs to the platinum-group metals. This is the Ashby chart redrawn as a map in which all five properties contribute at once, and in which the gaps between materials are filled by the decoder.

6 · Walking downhill

Because the decoder is differentiable, we can compute how an index changes when we nudge the code, and follow the gradient. Starting from a material, we update

z_{t+1} \;=\; z_t \;-\; \eta\,\nabla_z\Big[\log_{10} I_a\big(\mathrm{dec}(z)\big) \;+\; \lambda\,\lVert z\rVert^2\Big],

where the penalty $$\lambda\lVert z\rVert^2$$ keeps the walk inside the region the VAE has seen, since the prior says that codes far from the origin are rare. Latent-space optimisation of this kind is a standard way to propose new candidates in molecular and materials design . Our implementation uses the Adam optimiser and numerical gradients.

For the beam index every start converges to the same point, a production impact of about 6 mPt/kg, a density near 2 300 kg/m³ and a modulus around 25 GPa: the signature of concrete and natural stone, with limestone as the nearest real entry, although the readout of Figure 6 flags the end point as slightly outside the cloud of real materials. The decoded numbers are sensible and the conclusion matches the eco-Ashby chart. They are also a warning about what an index is: a beam in an aircraft would never be made of limestone, because the weight has its own environmental cost that this index leaves out. The optimiser answers the question we asked, not the one we meant.

7 · What the map cannot do

A VAE trained on 118 materials, five features and two latent dimensions is a visualisation device, not an oracle. We tested the most tempting use, imputing missing mechanical properties from the eco-profile alone: hide the density and modulus of a random 25 % of the materials, train on the rest, and ask the decoder to fill them in. Errors are RMSE in decades (log₁₀ units), averaged over four random splits:

guess the global mean0.611.39

guess the mean of the same family (a rule the VAE was not given)0.270.68

The VAE beats the global mean by 15–25 %, and loses clearly to a rule that knows the family. A one-decade error on a modulus is a useless prediction. The reason is physical: how much environmental burden a kilogram carries says little about how stiff it is. Three consequences for the course:

  • Low training error is memorisation. At β = 0.05 the model reconstructs the 118 training points almost exactly, which means nothing for a new material.
  • The map visualises and interpolates; it does not predict. The families cluster and the index fields are smooth, but a "material" synthesised by gradient search is an eco-profile to be checked against real data, never a recipe.
  • Inputs inherit the uncertainty of the booklet, which warns that small differences are not significant, and of our approximate ρ and E. A serious application would carry uncertainties through the model and use the booklet's "less trustworthy" grey flags.

8 · For the ecodesign course

The notebook notebook/ecolizer_vae.ipynb reproduces every number and figure. Some questions to take away:

  1. Why does changing the ReCiPe weights (400/400/200) change the ranking of lead, aluminium and wood but hardly the ranking of platinum?
  2. A product contains 2 kg of primary aluminium (Figure 1) and uses 200 kWh of electricity during its life (Figure 3). In which countries does the use phase outweigh the production of the aluminium? Does the answer change if the aluminium is secondary, from new scrap?
  3. Which materials in Figure 1 have the lowest recycling gain, and what does it tell you about where design for recycling pays off?
  4. In Figure 6, set λ = 0 and describe where the paths end. How far is that point from any real material? What would you say to a student who proposes it as a "target material"?
  5. Add σy for the metals and polymers and reproduce the strength eco-indices of Part 1 on the map.
